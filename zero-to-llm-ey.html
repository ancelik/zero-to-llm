The one sentence

It's autocomplete —
that had to learn what
the words mean.

You hand it some text; it predicts what comes next. Then it does it again. And again. That really is the whole machine.

But “just autocomplete” undersells it badly. To guess the next word well, it has no choice but to work out grammar, context, tone, and what the words actually refer to. You cannot finish this sentence without knowing what a cat is, and what sort of thing a cat sits on.

Under the hood

Four steps, on repeat

01
Tokens. Chop the text into chunks. "The cat sat" → [The][ cat][ sat]. Ours invents a private vocabulary of 4,096 chunks. GPT-4 used 100,277; GPT-4o and its successors use 200,019. Anthropic doesn't publish Claude's — which tells you it's considered worth keeping quiet.
02
Vectors. Turn each chunk into a long list of numbers, and place it in space. Words used alike land near each other — and, remarkably, the gaps between them carry meaning too. The step from man to woman is the same step as from king to queen. Nobody built that. It fell out of the guessing.
03
Attention. Every chunk looks back at the chunks before it, decides which matter, and rewrites its own vector to fold them in. For "the cat sat on the ___" the blank pulls hard on cat and sat. It's the vectors that change here — not the model's weights, which were fixed when it trained. This mix repeats through dozens of layers.
04
Probability. Score all 4,096 chunks. Pick one. Stick it on the end of the sentence. Then go back to step 1 and do the whole thing again.
Click through one at a time — the picture on the right changes with each step. This is the only genuinely technical slide in the deck, so let the visuals carry it and talk less.

Step 01, the honest aside: we know GPT's vocabularies exactly because OpenAI ships the tokenizer (100,277 for GPT-4; 200,019 for GPT-4o and after). Anthropic has never published Claude's. Worth a raised eyebrow — the tokenizer is a real piece of competitive engineering, and they treat it as such.

Step 02 is the one that matters. Slow down. The picture is the whole point: king − man + woman ≈ queen. The direction from man to woman is the same direction as king to queen — so the model has, entirely on its own, discovered something like the concept of gender, as a direction in space. And banana is nowhere near any of it.

Then say the thing that pays off the rest of the hour: "That is not a lookup table. That is a map of meaning — and in twenty minutes we are going to reach in and take Amazon's coordinates out of one."

Steps 03 and 04 can go quickly. If someone asks about attention: it's why the model handles "the bank of the river" differently from "the bank raised rates". Context changes the vector.
Training

Nobody teaches it anything

Take a pile of text. Hide the next word. Ask the model to guess. It guesses wrong. Calculus works out which of its millions of internal numbers caused the mistake, and nudges each one slightly in a better direction.

Repeat. Billions of times.

Grammar, style, world knowledge, the exact shape of a risk-factor disclosure — none of it is programmed. It all falls out of guess, get corrected, repeat.

When training stops, the numbers freeze. Run the frozen model in a loop and you get essays, code, or a very convincing 10-K.

the one number you'll watch

Loss = how wrong the guess was. Around 8, the model is guessing at random. Below 2, it has learned how a filing sounds. You'll watch it fall live — the training itself takes about twenty seconds.

This is the slide that makes the live demo legible. If they understand nothing else, they should know: loss goes down = it's working.

Analogy that lands: "It's a student who is given a billion sentences with the last word torn off, guesses each one, gets told the answer, and adjusts. Nobody ever explains grammar. After a billion rounds, they've inferred it."

Curve is a schematic of the expected shape — the notebook's own README says loss should fall from ~7–8 to ~3–4. Say that out loud so nobody thinks it's a fabricated result.
The bridge · pay attention to this one

The text it writes is the
boring part

To predict the next word in a document, the model is forced to build an internal picture of what that document is about. Not a summary — a list of 256 numbers. A fingerprint. That fingerprint is called an embedding.

For a model this small, the text it writes is a party trick — the embeddings are the real product. Every result in my research came out of these numbers, not out of a single word the model ever wrote.
Similarity

Which of these 9,438 filings read like this one? Not by keyword — by meaning.

Clustering

What themes are in this corpus that nobody has named yet?

Drift

How has this company's language changed over twenty years — and when did it turn?

This is the hinge of the whole talk. Slow down.

The intuition to give them: "In order to guess the next word of an audit report, the model has no choice but to work out — internally — what kind of document this is, what industry, what's going wrong, what tone. It has to understand it to predict it. We can reach in and take that understanding out as a list of numbers."

Then make it personal and specific rather than a vague appeal to the room: "Every number in the paper I'll show you in twenty minutes — the peer groups, the factor, the 4.57% — came out of these vectors. Not one of them came from anything the model wrote." The generation is a party trick. The embedding is the instrument.

Good closing line for the slide: "Chatbots are what language models are famous for. Embeddings are what they're useful for — and in empirical finance, they're the only part that survives contact with a t-statistic."

The problem that started this

Do investors read 10% profit the same way
at a grocer and at a software firm?

Same number. Opposite verdict. The question isn't whether you know that — it's whether the market knows it, and prices it.
Efficient markets say

A dollar of profit is a dollar. The market read the same filings you did, so any peer comparison is already in the price. Nothing left to find.

Behaviour says

Investors anchor on whichever reference group is handed to them. Open a Bloomberg terminal, look at the “comparable companies” panel. Somebody else chose that list. We don't know how, from what, or when it last changed. Everyone uses it anyway.

Where I think it sits

In between — and that's testable, not philosophical. Mostly priced; not entirely. Any residual shows up as a factor the five-factor model cannot explain.

Which turns it all on one question: compared to whom? The official answer is a code assigned decades ago — SIC — and it is broken two separate ways.
Set up a genuine disagreement, not a strawman. The efficient-markets answer is the respectable one and you should give it its due: the market has read the filings, the peer comparison is public information, so it should already be in the price. Say it like you believe it — because it's mostly right.

Then the behavioural point: people anchor on whatever reference group is handy. That's not a slur on investors; it's how judgement works under time pressure. You are talking to a room that benchmarks clients against peer sets for a living — they will feel this one.

Be careful to make the epistemic claim, not a factual one you can't back. Don't say the Bloomberg comps list is stale — you don't know that. Say the thing you do know, which is worse: "I cannot tell you how that list is constructed, what it was built from, or when it last changed. Neither can you. And every one of us has made a judgement off it." That's an audit question, and it will land in this room precisely because it's unanswerable.

Then land the middle ground, and make clear it's an empirical claim. "I'm not asserting the market is irrational. I'm asserting it's mostly efficient — and that the residual is measurable. If I'm wrong, the factor earns nothing and I go home." That framing is what makes the +4.57% alpha slide land later: you set up a falsifiable bet and then show the result.

Then make it non-finance for the room: "Every one of you asks 'compared to whom?' every day. Is this client's margin good — compared to whom? Is this fraud risk high — compared to what population? Peer selection is the hidden assumption underneath most professional judgement, and almost nobody audits it."
Why I couldn't just use an off-the-shelf model

Two problems, one of them fatal

Problem 1 · The label is stale

Amazon is still
filed as “mail order”

Roughly 90% of SIC codes never change once assigned. Amazon's is 5961 — mail order / catalog retail. Amazon also runs the largest cloud business on earth.

And firms are multi-faceted: Amazon is retail and cloud and advertising and logistics and healthcare. One static code cannot hold that.

Problem 2 · The model already knows the ending

You cannot ask Claude
what it didn't know

Ask any frontier model to describe Amazon as of 2010. It knows about AWS's rise, Whole Foods, the 2022 crash. It cannot un-know them.

That's look-ahead bias — you have smuggled the future into a model of the past. Harmless in a demo. Fatal in a backtest that moves money. No prompt removes it — it's baked into the weights. (Glasserman & Lin, 2024, document exactly this channel for pre-trained models in finance.)

There was no model on earth I could prove hadn't already seen the future. So I had to build one that couldn't have.
Problem 2 is the one to sell. It's counterintuitive and it's the honest reason you can't shortcut this with an API call.

Killer framing for EY: this is a provenance problem, and provenance is your native language. "You would never accept a valuation from an analyst who admits they might have peeked at next year's results. That is exactly what every off-the-shelf model is."

Expect a challenge here — "can't you just prompt it to ignore what it knows?" Answer: no. The knowledge is baked into the weights. You cannot instruct a model into amnesia, and you certainly cannot prove to a reviewer that it worked.

So what was a better peer group worth?

4.57% a year that the standard model cannot explain.

Finance has a standard scoreboard for what should drive a stock's return. Anything it can't explain is called alpha — return you were not supposed to be able to earn. I changed exactly one input on that scoreboard: compare a firm's profits to its text-identified peers rather than to the whole market. Nothing else.

What this actually says

Everyone already knew profitable firms tend to earn more — that has been priced for years. What nobody was pricing is being profitable relative to the right peers. That part was sitting there unclaimed.

And the honest boundary

Run the same peer trick on value instead of profitability and it falls apart out of sample. The effect is specific, not a free upgrade. A result that worked everywhere would be a bug, not a triumph.

Celik, N. (2026), “Profitability, Relative to Peers: Text-Identified Reference Groups and the Cross-Section of Returns.” Developed in Research Projects: Finance, taught by Eugene F. Fama, Chicago Booth.

This is the slide that justifies the whole talk — and the one where you are most likely to lose a non-finance room. Slow down and define the two words before you use them.

Alpha, in one breath: "There's a standard model that says what return a stock ought to give you for the risk you're taking. Alpha is what's left over — money the model says shouldn't be there." Sharpe, in one breath: "Return per unit of risk. Going from 0.63 to 0.88 means the same money working about forty percent harder."

Frame the change as minimal, because that's what makes it credible: "I didn't invent a new model. I took Fama and French's five-factor model and changed one input — who you compare a firm against. That's it."

Do not skip the boundary card. The same transform applied to value fails out of sample, and you say so. In a room full of auditors, volunteering the case where your method doesn't work buys you more credibility than any number on the left-hand side.

If someone asks "is this published?" — it's a Booth research project supervised by Fama, June 2026. Say exactly that. Don't oversell it.

Held in reserve — "isn't this just the profitability factor (RMW) again?" Any finance-literate person may ask; it doesn't need a slide, but you must be able to answer in one breath: "Rank firms on raw profitability and you basically get RMW back — my factor moves 0.84 with it. Rank them against their peers and it moves 0.26. Same data, same machinery; the only thing I changed is who you compare them to."

Those numbers are five-factor regression loadings on RMW: when RMW returns +1% in a month, the raw-built factor returns +0.84%, the peer-built one +0.26% — so 69% of the RMW exposure is stripped out, and it holds across all three windows (0.83→0.37 training, 0.91→0.32 train+validation, 0.84→0.26 whole sample). The two factors are not orthogonal and don't claim to be — that residual 0.26 is exactly the overlap, and it's small.

If they want the risk profile too (RMW against its own peer-relative twin): volatility 2.80% → 1.39%, return per unit of risk 0.41 → 0.73, and RMW's violent tails flatten (excess kurtosis 8.5 → 0.98). Half the swing, none of the crashes.
Now your turn

Four reasons to train your own model

01 · Sovereignty

The data that can't leave

Deal rooms. Working papers. Client files under privilege. A model you trained runs inside your perimeter and never phones home.

02 · Vocabulary

Fluent in your jargon, not the internet's

It won't write better than a frontier model — nothing this small will. It spends every parameter telling your documents apart. Worth testing. Not worth assuming.

03 · Defensibility

An answer that survives review

“The model said so” doesn't hold up in a review. Own the training set and you can state what went in — and prove what it could not have known.

04 · Economics

Cheap enough to be wrong

Twenty cents buys a toy. The thirty encoders behind that alpha came to a few thousand dollars — small enough that nobody had to approve it.

This is the slide they will photograph. Give it real time.

On 02, do not oversell. Someone in that room uses Claude or ChatGPT on domain text every day and knows perfectly well it's good at it. Claim that a 5M-parameter model beats a frontier model at reading a transfer-pricing memo and you lose them on the spot. Say the calibrated thing: "the off-the-shelf models are genuinely good here — this is a hypothesis to test on your own corpus, not a conclusion I'm handing you."

03 is the one that is uniquely yours. Everyone can talk about privacy and jargon. Almost nobody frames a model as an auditable artefact — but that is exactly what a firm whose entire product is assurance should care about. Provenance is a first-class deliverable here, not a compliance chore. Connect it explicitly back to equityAnalystYYYY.

On 04, do not oversell. Somebody in that room will do the arithmetic and realise a twenty-cent model can't do anything they'd bill for — and they'd be right. Get there first: "The model we built today is a toy. It does embeddings and it writes convincing nonsense. You could not sell it."

Then make the real point, which survives the scrutiny: "What changed isn't that useful models became free. It's that trying became free." A GPT-2-class model is about $50. The thirty encoders behind that alpha cost a research budget, not a lab's. You can now afford to be wrong three times before you're right — and that, not the twenty cents, is the thing that's new.

The part that's worth money

Now we throw the words away

We pushed all 9,438 company-years back through the model and kept only the internal fingerprint of each — 256 numbers per company. Not what it writes. What it understood. Then we flattened those onto a page.

Nobody labelled any of this

Every dot is one company-year. The model was shown text and nothing else — no industry codes, no sectors, no tickers. The clumps are its own.

Squeeze it and almost nothing is lost

Throw away 241 of the 256 numbers and you still keep 67% of everything that separates one company from another. The model gave itself 256 dimensions and only really needed about 15 — it found a handful of things that genuinely matter.

(That's about the shape of the space — not the number of clusters.)

t-SNE projection of a 2,169-point stratified sample; colours are k-means clusters found across all 9,438. Grey clusters are real too — just harder to name honestly.

Let the picture do the work. Say the one sentence that matters: "Nobody told it what a bank is."

These are k-means clusters on the raw embeddings, projected with t-SNE. The three named ones are the three I could honestly name by eye — banks, crypto trusts, biotech. The grey ones are genuine clusters too, but murkier, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Say that. A room of auditors will trust you more for it.

The 67% is easy to misread — I misread it myself at first, so assume the room will. It does NOT mean "67% of companies fall into 15 groups." It has nothing to do with clusters. It means: each company's fingerprint is 256 numbers, and if you threw away all but 15 of them you'd still keep two-thirds of what makes any two companies different.

Plain-English version if asked: "the model gave itself 256 dials and only really turned about 15 of them. It found a handful of things that actually matter, and ignored the rest." That's what "it found structure" means, mathematically — and it's why the embeddings are useful rather than noise.

01

Target screening

Screening tends to start from an industry code and somebody's comps list. An embedding builds the peer set from what a company actually does and fears — which might surface adjacent targets you hadn't screened for.

02

The data room

Nobody can read 4,000 contracts — but they can be ranked. It could surface the twelve that don't look like the other 3,988, collapse near-duplicates, and once a reviewer flags one ugly clause, find its cousins.

05

Two of everything

Every integration merges two vendor lists, two charts of accounts, two customer lists. An embedding can spot that two records are the same supplier even when the names look nothing alike — it compares meaning, not spelling. Whether that's worth doing is your call.

06

Synergy

The classic win is buying power — one merged buyer, better rates on the suppliers both sides already use. But first you have to spot which suppliers overlap, across two messy vendor lists.

07

Drift

You could embed both organisations' documents each quarter and watch whether they are actually converging — a number that might flag a stalling integration well before the EBITDA does.

This is the slide they will remember, so anchor it in their language, not yours. Walk the seven stages once, fast, then stop on the five.

Lead with 05, not 01. Screening is the glamorous story, but integration is where the money is and where nobody expects a model. Ask the room how they match two vendor masters today; the answer is usually a junior person and a spreadsheet. "Is ACME CORPORATION in their system the same supplier as Acme Corp Ltd in ours?" That is a similarity problem, which is exactly what an embedding is.

02 is triage, not reading. Be precise: the model doesn't read the contracts for you. It ranks them, so the strange ones surface before the human hours run out.

07 is the one nobody has thought of. A convergence number, eighteen months before the EBITDA tells you the same thing.
